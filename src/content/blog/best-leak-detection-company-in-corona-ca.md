## What should a leak detection visit actually include?

A proper leak detection visit uses non-invasive equipment to locate the leak before anyone opens a wall or cuts concrete. That typically means acoustic listening devices, infrared thermal imaging, and pressure testing on the plumbing system.

Acoustic equipment picks up the sound of water escaping under pressure, even through concrete or drywall. Thermal imaging shows temperature differences in a wall or floor, which often reveal a wet area invisible to the eye. Pressure testing isolates sections of the plumbing system to confirm whether the leak is on the supply side or the drain side, which changes the repair entirely. A technician who shows up with only a moisture meter and a guess is skipping steps that matter.

## Leak detection only vs. locate-and-repair: which do you need?

Leak detection only means the technician finds and documents the leak's location and gives you that information to hand to a plumber or contractor. Locate-and-repair means the same company finds it and fixes it, usually at a lower total cost than paying two separate businesses.

For a simple exposed-pipe leak, detection-only from a plumber can make sense. But when the leak has been running long enough to soak insulation, subfloor, or drywall, which is common with slab leaks that go unnoticed for weeks, you also need someone who can assess and dry that damage under the [IICRC S500 water restoration standard](https://iicrc.org/s500/). That's the gap a plumbing-only leak specialist can't close on their own, and it's the reason to ask about damage assessment before you book, not after.

## What should you ask before you book a leak detection visit?

Ask these four questions before scheduling, and get the answers in writing. A company that hesitates on any of them is telling you something.

- **Is there a diagnostic fee, and does it apply toward repair if you move forward?**
- **What non-invasive equipment will the technician actually use** (acoustic, thermal, pressure test), not just "we'll find it"?
- **Will the quote separate the locate cost from the repair cost**, so you can see each line item?
- **Is the company licensed?** You can verify any California contractor's license status directly through the [CSLB license lookup](https://www.cslb.ca.gov/OnlineServices/CheckLicenseII/CheckLicense.aspx) before you let anyone into your home, and the [CSLB's own hiring guide](https://www.cslb.ca.gov/Consumers/Hire_A_Contractor/) walks through what else to confirm.

## What happens after the leak is found?

Once the leak is located, the next steps are moisture mapping, drying, and insurance documentation, in that order. Skipping any of them is how a $400 leak turns into a $4,000 mold problem.

Moisture mapping uses a penetrating moisture meter to chart exactly how far the water traveled through the wall, subfloor, or slab, not just where the pipe broke. That map determines what gets dried in place and what needs to come out. Standing water from a supply line starts as Category 1 (clean) water, but once it sits against drywall or insulation for more than a day or two, it no longer behaves like clean water and needs to be treated accordingly. If drying isn't handled by the same visit, document everything with photos; most homeowners insurance policies cover sudden, accidental water damage from a burst or failed line, but typically exclude damage from a leak that was gradual or went unaddressed, so [what your policy actually covers](https://www.iii.org/article/what-is-covered-by-standard-homeowners-insurance) is worth confirming before repairs start. This is also where [water damage restoration](/services/water-damage-restoration/) picks up from leak detection, mapping the affected area, setting drying equipment, and producing the documentation an adjuster will ask for. The [EPA's WaterSense program](https://www.epa.gov/watersense) also has good guidance on catching small leaks early, before they become this kind of job.
