## What should you do in the first five minutes?

Shut the cold water supply valve on top of the tank, then kill the gas valve or the breaker feeding the heater, before you touch anything else. That stops the flood from growing and removes the fire or shock risk while help is on the way.

The cold supply valve is usually a small oval or lever handle on the pipe entering the top of the tank; turn it clockwise until it stops. For a gas unit, turn the gas control knob to "off" at the base. For an electric unit, go to the panel and flip the dedicated breaker, usually labeled "water heater." If standing water is anywhere near an outlet, light switch, or the electrical panel itself, stay out of it and shut off power from a dry spot first, or wait for someone who can.

## Do you call a plumber, a restoration crew, or both?

A plumber replaces or repairs the tank itself; a restoration crew removes the standing water and dries the structure underneath and around it, and most Corona homes with a tank failure need both. Skipping the second call is the most common reason a "fixed" leak turns into a mold problem a few weeks later.

The plumber's scope ends at the appliance: isolating the line, draining what's left in the tank, and installing the replacement unit or repair part. Any licensed California plumbing contractor should carry a current [CSLB license you can verify online](https://www.cslb.ca.gov/OnlineServices/CheckLicenseII/CheckLicense.aspx) before they touch your plumbing. The restoration side is a separate scope: extracting water from the slab or subfloor, mapping moisture with a meter behind drywall and baseboards, and running drying equipment set up to the [ANSI/IICRC S500 standard](https://iicrc.org/s500/) for water damage restoration. When one company handles both, as ours does, you get one scope of work and one point of contact instead of two invoices and two schedules to coordinate. ## Does it matter whether the heater sits in a garage or an interior closet?

Location changes where the water travels, not whether you need drying. On a garage slab, water spreads fast across the concrete and wicks up into the base of the drywall at the wall line; in an interior closet, it soaks the subfloor and baseboards and often shows up under the flooring in the room next door before anyone notices.

A garage floor that looks dry an hour later can still be a problem. Concrete sheds surface water quickly, but drywall paper and the wood baseboard behind it pull moisture upward through capillary action, long after the visible puddle is gone. That's why a moisture meter reading at the base of the wall matters more than how the floor looks. For a closet install, the same wicking happens into the subfloor, and if the closet backs onto a bedroom or hallway, that's often where the carpet or laminate swells first.

## What if the water heater is in an attic or an upstairs closet?

Water from an attic or upstairs unit runs downhill through insulation and drywall, so the real damage usually shows up as a stained ceiling or a soft spot on the floor below, not at the tank itself. By the time a ceiling stain appears, insulation above it has typically been wet for a while.

That delay matters because mold can begin growing on wet material within [24 to 48 hours](https://www.epa.gov/mold/brief-guide-mold-moisture-and-your-home). An attic or upstairs leak needs the ceiling drywall checked and, in most cases, opened in a contained area so the cavity above can actually dry, not just the visible stain painted over.

## What should you photograph before anything gets moved?

Photograph the tank's data plate (age and serial number), the water line on walls and flooring, and anything sitting in the water, before cleanup starts and before anything gets thrown away. Adjusters use these photos to confirm the cause and the extent of the loss.

Get a wide shot of each affected room, then close-ups of the tank base, the corrosion or rupture point if visible, and the water line on baseboards. Homeowners insurance typically covers sudden, accidental tank failure, but usually excludes damage from a slow leak or years of visible corrosion, so photos of the failure point matter for how the claim gets classified.
