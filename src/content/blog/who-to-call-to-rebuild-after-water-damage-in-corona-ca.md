## Mitigation vs. Reconstruction: Who Do You Actually Call?

Mitigation and reconstruction are two different jobs, and mixing them up is the most common reason a rebuild stalls. Mitigation is the drying phase: extracting standing water, running air movers and dehumidifiers, and documenting that the structure has returned to a dry standard. Reconstruction is everything that happens after: new drywall, texture, paint, flooring, cabinetry, and trim.

If you already worked through the drying phase, you've likely seen our [Who to Call for Water Damage Restoration in Corona, CA](/blog/who-to-call-for-water-damage-restoration-in-corona-ca/) post, which covers that first call. This post picks up where that one leaves off. Some mitigation-only companies hand you a dry certificate and walk away, leaving you to find a separate general contractor. ## Why Your Rebuild Can't Start Until the Drying Numbers Are Signed Off

A reconstruction crew should never close up a wall until the mitigation technician has documented dry readings with a moisture meter, because trapped moisture behind new drywall is how a one-week repair turns into a mold job. The [ANSI/IICRC S500 standard](https://iicrc.org/s500/) is the industry benchmark drying technicians use to confirm materials have returned to a normal moisture range before reconstruction begins.

Ask for that documentation in writing before anyone picks up a drill. It protects you three ways: it gives your rebuild contractor a defensible starting point, it gives your insurer proof the structure was actually dry, and it protects you if a wall gets reopened down the road and someone asks what the readings were on closing day.

## What Does the Rebuild Actually Cover?

Rebuilding after water damage in a Corona home typically means replacing drywall, repairing or replacing ceiling sections, matching texture and paint, and addressing flooring and cabinetry that absorbed water. Ceilings are their own category of headache: a slow leak from a second-floor bathroom or an attic HVAC line often shows up as a stained or sagging ceiling long after the original leak, which is why we treat [ceiling water damage repair](/services/ceiling-water-damage-repair/) as its own line item rather than a drywall afterthought.

Cabinets and baseboards near the original water source usually need removal during drying and reinstallation (or replacement) during reconstruction. Flooring depends on the material: vinyl plank and tile often survive if dried quickly, while particleboard subfloor and swollen laminate usually don't. Paint and texture matching is the last step, and it's where a lot of rebuilds go sideways if the contractor doesn't blend the repair into the surrounding wall or ceiling.

## What About the Adjuster's Estimate and Supplements?

The adjuster's first estimate is a starting point, not a final number, and it's common for the actual rebuild cost to exceed it once walls are open and hidden damage is visible. That gap gets closed through a supplement: additional documentation your contractor submits to the insurer showing the extra scope and cost once it's discovered.

This is where a contractor who understands insurance paperwork earns their keep. The [Insurance Information Institute's guide to settling claims after a disaster](https://www.iii.org/article/settling-insurance-claims-after-a-disaster) walks through how the claims process generally works, including the back-and-forth between contractor estimates and adjuster approval. Dry County writes a documented scope before work begins and handles supplement requests directly with the adjuster so you're not the one chasing paperwork between two offices.

## How Do I Check a Contractor's License Before I Sign?

Any contractor rebuilding your home after water damage in California should hold an active CSLB license, and you can verify it yourself in under a minute at the [CSLB's online license lookup](https://www.cslb.ca.gov/OnlineServices/CheckLicenseII/CheckLicense.aspx). Enter the license number from the estimate or contract and confirm the status is active, the classification matches the work (general building, not just a specialty trade), and there's no current disciplinary action.

The [CSLB's own guide to hiring a contractor](https://www.cslb.ca.gov/Consumers/Hire_A_Contractor/) recommends getting a written contract with a defined scope before any demolition or rebuild work starts, which matters even more when insurance money is involved.
