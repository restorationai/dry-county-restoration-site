Most Corona tract homes were built slab-on-grade, which means the copper or PEX supply lines run under the concrete floor instead of through a crawlspace. When one of those lines pinholes or splits, the water doesn't drip into a visible spot. It spreads sideways under the slab and works its way up through flooring, baseboards, and drywall before you ever see it.

## What are the signs of a slab leak?

The most common signs are a warm patch on the flooring, the sound of running water when every fixture in the house is off, and a water bill that jumps with no obvious cause. Cracked tile, buckling laminate, or a damp carpet seam near an interior wall are also typical.

A warm spot usually points to a leak on the hot-water line, since the escaping water heats the slab above it. A cold or simply damp spot can mean a leak on the cold line or in the sewer lateral instead. Either way, the sound test is the easiest first check: shut off every faucet, ice maker, and irrigation valve, then watch the water meter's small leak-indicator dial. If it's still spinning, water is moving somewhere in the system.

## Who do I call first: a plumber, a leak detection specialist, or a restoration company?

Call a leak detection specialist when the water is hidden, and call an emergency plumber or restoration company right away when water is already visible or actively flowing. The order matters because cutting into the wrong spot in a slab is expensive and often unnecessary.

If you can see standing water or a wet carpet edge and don't yet know the source, start with an emergency plumber or a restoration company that also does leak detection, so the shutoff and the search happen on the same visit. If there's no visible water but the signs above are present, a dedicated leak detection technician uses acoustic listening equipment, thermal imaging, and tracer gas to pinpoint the leak's location without guesswork, often within a few feet, before anyone touches the concrete.

## What should I do before anyone arrives?

Shut off the water at the main valve if you can see or hear active water, and take photos of the affected area before you move anything. That protects both your home and your insurance claim.

Don't try to pull up flooring or cut into the slab yourself; locating the exact leak point first avoids opening the wrong section of concrete. Move rugs, furniture, and electronics off the wet area if it's safe to do so, and keep your water bill and any utility notices; a documented spike in usage is useful evidence if you file a claim.
